Economic activity is increasingly operated by a new class of actors: LLM-powered agents making decisions autonomously. These activities range from business negotiations to group coordination, raising critical questions about their impact: are these agents a boon to performance or a bane to human dynamics? We investigate these questions in an online behavioral experiment (N = 459) using a multi-player, multi-turn bargaining game. Participants were randomly assigned to one of three conditions: (1) no AI assistance, (2) AI-generated recommendations (Advisor), or (3) full AI delegation (Delegate). We find that delegation is a significant boon: participants using the Delegate achieve significantly higher gains than those in the Advisor and no-AI conditions. Moreover, delegation generates positive externalities; even non-adopting human players in Delegate-enabled games benefit by receiving higher-quality offers. However, the benefits are heterogeneous, revealing a potential bane for certain users. User profiles predict divergent outcomes: “delegate-preferrers” exhibit high self-confidence yet underperform substantially without AI, while “autonomy-seekers” prefer minimal assistance and perform well independently. These findings highlight a critical tension between the objective gains of AI delegation and the subjective preferences and abilities of human users.
